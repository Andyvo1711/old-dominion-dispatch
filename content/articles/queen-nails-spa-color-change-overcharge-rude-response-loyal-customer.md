A longtime customer of Queen Nails & Spa said her most recent visit left her rethinking her loyalty to a salon she had trusted for reasonable prices and quality work. What was meant to be a simple color change turned into a pricing dispute, and the way it was handled left an even bigger impression than the bill itself.

## A Pricing Surprise at the Counter

The client said she came in for a color change, a service she expected to cost around $35 based on her past visits. When she reached the counter, she was told the total would be closer to $65, in line with what a new full set would cost. She raised the discrepancy, feeling the price didn't match the service she had received, and said the owner's wife responded in a way she found rude rather than working to clarify the confusion. For a salon built on repeat business, unexpected charges at checkout put staff in a difficult position, especially with regulars who have certain price points in mind from experience. Posting clear pricing, or explaining any add-on charges before the service begins rather than after, gives both the client and the front desk a much easier conversation to have.

## Damage During Removal and an Unsatisfying Resolution

The client also described a separate issue with the technician who worked on her that day, saying that while removing her color, he took off much of the acrylic underneath in the process, leaving her natural nails visibly thinner. She said she expects the nails will not hold up well given how much was removed. When she followed up about the pricing, she was offered a $5 discount, which she described as insulting given the trouble the visit had caused. She also noted that this had never been an issue when working with a technician she'd previously trusted at the salon, someone she felt communicated pricing far more clearly. A modest discount rarely resolves a client's frustration when the underlying issues, unclear pricing and nail damage, remain unaddressed. Listening closely to what a client is actually asking for, whether that's an explanation, a fix, or a more meaningful gesture, tends to matter more than the dollar amount offered.

## Editorial Perspective

First, pricing should be communicated to the client before a service begins, not discovered at the register, especially for anything that could be mistaken for a routine service. Second, staff responses to a customer's concern should stay calm and solution-focused, even when the customer is visibly frustrated, since a defensive reaction can turn a pricing question into a lost customer. Third, technique matters as much as pricing; care should be taken during removal services so a client's natural nails aren't compromised in the process of correcting or undoing previous work.
