A client who brings a reference photo to her appointment is telling her technician exactly what she hopes to see at the end. A recent review of Star Nail comes from a customer who did just that and left feeling the result was not what she had asked for. Her account touches on pacing, design accuracy, and the importance of consistency from visit to visit, all of which are within a team's control.

## A Quiet Salon and a Fast Pace

The customer noted that when she arrived, there were not many people in the salon. Even so, she felt the technician seemed to be in a hurry. This is an observation worth pausing on, because pace is one of the first things a guest notices. When a salon is quiet, clients expect an unhurried, attentive experience, and a fast pace can feel out of step with the moment.

A relaxed greeting, a short chat about what the client has in mind, and a steady working rhythm help set the tone. Even on busy days, slowing down for the first minute of a service tends to save time later, because it reduces the chance of redoing work.

## Matching the Reference Photo

The reviewer says she spent $77 on nails that looked "completely different" from the picture she showed. She added that she could have bought press-on nails that looked better for less. That comparison is a useful reminder of how clients measure value: they compare the finished result to the image in their head and to the alternatives available to them.

A reference photo works best when it is discussed at the start. The technician can look at the image with the client, point out key features such as length, shape, color, and design placement, and say what is achievable. If part of the design is not possible with the time or products available, it is better to explain it up front and offer the closest alternative. Another good habit is to hold the photo next to the finished hand before the client leaves, so any differences can be corrected right away.

## Pressure, Pace, and the Pedicure Offer

The customer described the technician as heavy handed and moving too quickly, and she said that when a pedicure was offered, her answer was an immediate no. Touch pressure is a very personal matter, and clients can feel uneasy when a service seems rough, even when no harm is done. A simple "Is this pressure okay for you?" at the start of the manicure and again partway through helps clients feel in control.

It is also worth noting how the pedicure suggestion landed. Offering an additional service can be welcome when the guest feels comfortable, but it is much less effective when the client is already uncertain about the quality of the work. Building confidence first is the best way to earn the next sale.

Finally, the reviewer mentioned that her preferred technician was busy, that she plans to return for that person next time, and that her experiences have been "a hit or miss." Consistency is what clients are really looking for. Teams can support it by sharing client preferences through notes, using common checklists for steps, and offering a simple way to book with a favorite technician.

## Editorial Perspective

This case offers three lessons for nail teams. First, review the reference photo together at the start, explain what is achievable, and compare the finished set to the photo before the client leaves. Second, keep a calm, steady pace and check in on pressure during the service, especially when the salon is not busy. Third, aim for consistency across technicians through shared notes and standard steps, so that every visit meets the same quality, whoever is at the table.

Clients return to a salon when they can count on the result. Turning every visit into a "hit" is the surest way to keep them.
